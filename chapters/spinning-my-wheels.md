title: Spinning My Wheels
permalink: /spinning-my-wheels/

After this season of rapid spiritual growth, I found myself spinning my wheels once again. Worse yet, I found myself back in the same cycle of rededication that I had experienced in youth group.

Think about it. Here I was, getting spiritually charged up at a retreat. Yes, I was an adult now. Yes, the topics were different. Instead of hearing about teenage temptations and peer pressure, we were talking about marriage, fatherhood, work, and the responsibilities of adulthood. But the effect was remarkably similar. I would experience a spiritual high, only to find myself drifting back into a slump afterward. Maybe I wasn't falling as far down the mountain as I had in the past, and perhaps there is something to be said for that. Still, the cycle was back in full force. In fact, it would only become more frustrating when I later became a youth leader and began to feel, like George Jetson, that I wanted to "get off this crazy thing."

I was no longer content to sit at the bottom of the mountain waiting for the next spiritual high, but I had no idea what to do differently. The Thursday night men's group was good and beneficial, but I had virtually no personal prayer life. Even during our Thursday gatherings, prayer often seemed shortened in favor of extended Bible discussion.

I don't say that with bitterness or criticism. In many ways, it is simply what happens when there is no liturgical tradition of prayer. When every prayer is spontaneous and composed in the moment, it is easy to run out of things to say. You find yourself repeating the same themes and petitions. It is not necessarily a flaw in the system so much as a feature of it.

Wanting to learn how to pray more deeply, I began looking for guidance. I read books on prayer and explored various traditions. For a time, I looked at Catholic prayer books, but eventually I settled on a book produced by the Anglican Church in North America called the Book of Common Prayer. It was a modernized adaptation of the classic prayer book of the Church of England.

I began reading the prescribed prayers each morning and evening. One thing immediately stood out to me: these prayers were substantial. They included opening sentences, confessions of sin, hymns and canticles, praises, Scripture readings, creeds, and other elements. Sometimes it would take me half an hour or more to complete the service. Rather than seeing that as a drawback, I welcomed it. I was actively looking for ways to extend my prayer life, and this seemed like a meaningful path forward.

For months, I struggled to make daily prayer a consistent habit. I even invited my family to join me using the Family Prayer section of the book. Unfortunately, it never really took root. I don't know whether that was due to my own lack of discipline, the absence of a community practicing these things alongside me, or the fact that my family never fully embraced it. Perhaps it was a combination of all three. Whatever the reason, I simply couldn't establish the habit.

And yet, even in that failure, I sensed that there was truth in what I was discovering. There was something here that resonated with a deep longing in my soul. It felt more worthy of God, more reverent and substantial than the prayers I could spontaneously generate on my own, which often consisted primarily of thanking God for things or asking Him for things. These ancient prayers gave voice to desires, fears, and acts of worship that I often felt but could never adequately express.

Looking back, this was one of the first steps on a road that would eventually lead me toward the One, Holy, Catholic, and Apostolic Church.

I spent a great deal of time trying different things to improve my spiritual life. It was frustrating that my own tradition seemed so limited in the tools it offered for spiritual growth beyond the familiar refrain: "Read your Bible and pray." While those are certainly essential, I found myself longing for something more. At the same time, it was equally frustrating to look outside my tradition and see what felt like an endless buffet of spiritual practices, books, and methods. I was left to sort through it all myself, trying to determine what was helpful, what was harmful, and what would actually draw me closer to Christ.

Looking back, I don't think I was alone in this experience. Many within the Christian and Missionary Alliance seemed to be searching outside our tradition for ways to deepen their spiritual lives. Over time, books, speakers, and new ministry approaches began finding their way into our churches. These often drew from the same buffet of practices that I was exploring. There were books on contemplative prayer, inner healing and deliverance ministries such as Soul Care that shared similarities with Sozo-style approaches, and charismatic practices such as speaking in tongues and other spiritual gifts.

None of these became the central focus of our churches, nor were they regularly preached from the pulpit on Sunday mornings. Yet many people within the denomination were becoming interested in them, myself included. We were searching for something deeper, something that would move beyond mere knowledge and into a living experience of God.

Looking back now, I understand the desire that drove so many of us. People genuinely want an authentic spiritual life. They want to know God, not merely know about Him. And when they cannot find a clear path within their own tradition, they often become willing to venture into unfamiliar territory in search of that deeper relationship.

But unfamiliar territory can also be spiritually dangerous. Not everything that promises closeness to God actually leads to Him. In my own experience, and especially through some of the things I witnessed during my years in youth ministry, I came to see that sincere spiritual hunger can sometimes lead people into practices that are spiritually and physically harmful. As I share more stories from that period of my life, I think I will be vindicated in saying so.
