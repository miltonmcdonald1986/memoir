title: Fear

Fear played a significant role in shaping my spirituality as a child. I was explicitly taught to be afraid of many things: Satanism, demons, witchcraft, the occult, Halloween, trick‑or‑treating. I frequently heard that people hid razor blades in Halloween candy or that we might encounter real witches and demons if we participated. Rock and roll was called “the devil’s music,” and we heard sensational stories about rock musicians engaging in satanic rituals or other shocking behavior. Ouija boards were seen as doorways to demonic possession. I’m not saying all of this was necessarily untrue, but it was the fear that got to me—the sense that danger was always lurking somewhere and that I was never quite safe. Looking back, I can appreciate why the adults around me wanted to protect me from what they believed was harmful. What affected me most, however, was not the warnings themselves but the constant atmosphere of fear that surrounded them.

A couple of stories in particular illustrate what I mean. These were the kinds of things that circulated first through chain letters and later through email, social media, and YouTube.

One story that frightened me involved the Black‑Eyed Children, mysterious figures said to knock on doors at night. I was told that as long as I never let them into my home, I would be safe, but if they were allowed inside, something terrible would happen. What made the story especially frightening was that these children were said to prey on a person's compassion. They would beg for food, claim someone was chasing them, or ask for shelter. According to the story, that was how they lured people into letting them in.

I was terrified of these children and desperately hoped I would never encounter them. It wasn't until years later that I learned the story originated in a tabloid account from the 1980s. Its origins had nothing to do with Christian teaching; it was simply an urban legend that had found its way into religious circles. But the fear it produced in me was real.

Another story that circulated in my circles was the Three Days of Darkness, a prophecy that claimed the sun would go dark for three days. We were told that every window had to be completely covered or demonic forces could enter the home and bring harm. No artificial light would work, only blessed beeswax candles, and families were encouraged to stockpile food and supplies to survive the event. Years later, I was surprised to learn that the prophecy was commonly traced back to the private revelations of the Roman Catholic mystic Anna Maria Taigi. According to these accounts, the faithful were instructed to remain indoors, cover their windows, and pray while darkness, pestilence, and spiritual forces roamed outside.

Looking back, I found the whole thing puzzling. The religious environment in which I was raised was strongly anti-Catholic and generally dismissed Catholic teachings and traditions. Yet this particular story seemed to circulate freely despite its Catholic origins. I don't bring this up to ridicule anyone. Rather, it illustrates how fear can sometimes override our usual attitudes.

The fears that stayed with me the longest were not the urban legends themselves. As I grew older, the deeper fear was the fear of being lost. Even after I had prayed for salvation and sincerely committed my life to Christ, I was never quite free from the question of whether I was truly safe. I remember passing billboards that asked, "If you died today, do you know where you would spend eternity?" Churches would sometimes present dramatic skits in which teenagers rebelled, died unexpectedly in a car crash, and then faced the judgment of God. As a teenager and young adult struggling to follow Christ amid the temptations and failures of ordinary life, those messages never felt theoretical. The possibility of being wrong before God always seemed close at hand.

The underlying message I absorbed from all of this was simple: danger was everywhere, and I was always one wrong step away from going to hell. I felt like I was constantly bracing myself for some terrible event that never actually happened, whether it was the rapture, the Three Days of Darkness, or razor blades hidden in my Halloween candy. I didn't fully recognize this until many years later, but there seemed to be a strange contradiction at work. On Sunday we would sing Blessed Assurance, declaring confidence in Christ, and then spend the rest of the week worrying that one wrong decision, action, or belief could place us in spiritual danger. Assurance was something we professed, but fear often seemed to have a greater influence on my spiritual formation. Looking back, I found it difficult to develop a healthy view of God because my sense of security often seemed to hinge on whether I was believing sincerely enough, repenting sincerely enough, or trusting sincerely enough.

Since becoming Orthodox, I've had people tell me that I've "left behind assurance." Much of this comes from the fact that the Orthodox Church does not presume to declare with certainty who will ultimately be saved and who will not. We believe that Christ is the final judge and that such judgments belong to Him alone. For this reason, some people criticize Orthodoxy for lacking assurance, arguing that Orthodox Christians cannot know whether they are going to heaven. I don't think that is quite accurate. While we would not presume to make such declarations, we do trust in the love and mercy of Christ, seek to follow Him faithfully, and desire to be united with Him. Our hope ultimately rests not in ourselves but in Him.

Over time, I've come to have significant reservations about the doctrine of assurance as it was presented to me. In the tradition I grew up in, assurance was often closely connected to a particular style of evangelism that emphasized making a decisive commitment to Christ and then resting confidently in that decision. While I understand the appeal of this approach, I became concerned that it could sometimes be interpreted in a way that encouraged spiritual complacency rather than ongoing repentance and growth. I also struggled with the fact that those who lacked assurance were often encouraged to examine whether they were believing sincerely enough or trusting deeply enough. The focus seemed to turn inward, toward the quality of my own faith, rather than outward toward the mercy of Christ. Fear can serve a useful purpose if it awakens someone to their need for God and leads them to repentance. But I increasingly found myself asking whether fear was meant to remain after that initial turning to Christ. In my own experience, it did. Perhaps others experienced this differently, but I found little comfort in constantly evaluating the sincerity of my own belief. If my experience is any indication, people who confidently profess assurance do not always feel as certain inwardly as they appear. Or maybe it was just me.

One of the things that drew me to Orthodoxy was that it offered a way of following Christ that was neither presumption nor despair. Rather than constantly asking whether I had believed hard enough, prayed sincerely enough, or met some invisible standard, I was given a path to walk. The Church provided a framework larger than my own thoughts and emotions, allowing me to submit both my fears and my confidence to God. I did not find certainty about my own spiritual status, but I did find the freedom to follow Christ in humble hope rather than constant fear.
