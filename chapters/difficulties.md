One of the hardest things I had to work through was the Orthodox veneration of the saints, especially the Theotokos. In my evangelical worldview, even though it was never stated outright, praise and worship functioned like a zero‑sum game: any honor given to someone else must be honor taken away from God. So the idea of praying to a saint or showing reverence for his holiness felt wrong, because that kind of attention seemed like it should belong to God alone. I often heard the familiar objection, “Why pray to Mary or the saints when you can go straight to God?” It sounds reasonable until you remember that Christians ask each other for intercession constantly. If I asked a Christian friend, “Will you pray for me?” he would never respond, “Why ask me when you can go straight to God?” So the real difficulty wasn’t intercession itself, but the intercession of Christians who have gone before us.

This is where evangelicals and Orthodox Christians often talk past each other. I once mentioned to an evangelical pastor that I thought it was beautiful how Christians ask the saints to intercede for them, and he immediately compared it to necromancy—like Saul summoning Samuel through the witch of Endor. But that’s not what Orthodox Christians believe they’re doing. They aren’t trying to conjure the dead; they’re speaking to the living. Christ Himself said that God is not the God of the dead, but of the living. That was enough for me to accept the idea of asking the saints for their prayers. What remained difficult was the veneration of them.

This was extremely hard to untangle because I still viewed honor, veneration, worship, and praise through the zero‑sum lens I had inherited. My perspective only began to change when I learned the difference between veneration and worship—and when I realized that most of what happens in evangelical services is not actually worship but veneration of God. Worship, in the biblical sense, always involves sacrifice. If all you ever do is venerate God, it becomes difficult to understand how venerating the saints doesn’t steal glory from Him. That confusion is the natural result of a tradition cut off from the historic worship of the Church for the first 1,500 years.

It was also the result of not understanding what a kingdom looks like. In a kingdom, honor is dynamic. The king delights to bestow honors on his faithful servants. The queen mother holds a place of unique dignity. But as Americans, we are often blind to this kind of structure. As I have studied the Bible through the lens of the Holy Fathers, the basis for intercession and veneration has been opened up to me, especially through passages in Revelation, Hebrews, and Psalms. My hesitation was sincere, but it was misguided.

None of this was easy. It took months of personal struggle, and I prayed constantly that I wouldn’t accidentally do something that offended God. But slowly, the pieces began to fit together. As it says in Hebrews "But ye are come unto mount Sion, and unto the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem, and to an innumerable company of angels, to the general assembly and church of the firstborn, which are written in heaven, and to God the Judge of all, and to the spirits of just men made perfect, and to Jesus the mediator of the new covenant, and to the blood of sprinkling, that speaketh better things than that of Abel. See that ye refuse not him that speaketh." If I was wanting to live in the kingdom of God, I had to accept a kingdom mindset.

Another difficult issue for me to work through was the veneration of icons in the Orthodox Church. At first glance, creating images of Christ, the saints, angels, and biblical events—and then bowing, prostrating, or kissing them—appears to violate the Second Commandment. That was my initial assumption. But Scripture contains several places where God explicitly commands the creation of images (such as in Exodus), where the heavenly temple includes images (Ezekiel 41), and where people bow before the Ark (Psalm 99), bow to one another, and even bow before angels. When Scripture rebukes bowing, it is because someone mistakenly gives divine adoration to a human or an angel. Ordinary bowing, as an act of veneration, is not criticized.

I also encountered the incarnational theology of images: the idea that Christ’s coming in the flesh fulfills the Second Commandment because God has now been seen and can be depicted. For me, this was enough to show that the commandment must be read in context. I had reached a point where I trusted the interpretation of the Church Fathers more than my own private reading of Scripture. Submitting my own ideas to the Church was humbling—especially since I tend to see myself as one of the smart people in the room—but it was freeing to rely on the Fathers who have handed down the faith for generations.

And I won’t deny that I’ve learned to trust my instincts on these matters. When I look at icons of the martyrs, the saints, Christ, and the Theotokos, I recognize something true and good. You might argue that I am mistaken. Very well. But that does not change my perspective.

There were other issues I had to deal with, and none of it was easy. So many of the biases built into me from my youth had to be overcome, but I finally came to the point where I recognized the truth and the fullness of the faith as experienced in Eastern Orthodoxy. And all this was happening in parallel to my soul-crushing experiences my third year of being a youth leader, all the charismatic insanity, the unauthorized baptism, the trip to Indianapolis, and more. I was now Orthodox in my heart, at least I hoped I was. The only problem was, I had never stepped foot inside an Orthodox Church, but that was about to change.
