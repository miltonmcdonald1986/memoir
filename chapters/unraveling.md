The third year of leading youth marked the beginning of the end for me. Several key events left me completely disillusioned with evangelical religion and searching for a way out. It really was the culmination of a lifelong spiritual struggle, and it all came to a head that year. It began normally enough—over thirty students were coming regularly, which strained my energy and made teaching harder, especially as more younger kids joined. Thankfully, a few additional leaders stepped in to help. But the vibe was shifting, and the rededication cycle wasn’t fueling me the way it used to. The highs weren’t as high. I started to wonder if I had become a kind of spiritual addict, always chasing the next emotional hit. Still, we made it through the fall as usual.

Then came the retreat from hell.

Four days before the retreat, I had surgery to repair a torn meniscus in my right knee, but I was determined to be there. My wife promised to drive me from building to building so I could rest in a chair all weekend. A major snowstorm hit, making everything harder. The retreat began normally enough. The speaker was intense—aggressive, even—and his stories of miracles left us uneasy, especially since they directly contradicted the seminar I had taught earlier that day. He claimed the Holy Spirit had struck him like a lightning bolt and instantly removed his worst sins. My lesson had been the opposite: there is no lightning bolt; we die daily, take up our cross, and follow Christ. My co‑leaders agreed something felt off, but nothing seemed outright dangerous. The kids were having fun, and we tried to stay optimistic.

Everything changed on Saturday night.

I was in so much pain that I lay on the floor in the back of the room during the message. The speaker talked for nearly three hours with soft keyboard music playing behind him the entire time. He planted manipulative suggestions in the kids’ minds—“If you feel electricity in your fingers, stand and I’ll pray for you,” “If you feel the breath of the Holy Spirit on your cheek, stand and I’ll pray for you.” What do you expect a room full of exhausted, hyped‑up middle schoolers to do? Of course they started “feeling” things. He never preached the gospel, just told story after story of miracles he had supposedly performed in other youth groups. I realized he was full of nonsense, but I didn’t want to cause a scene. I just hoped he would wrap up soon.

Because I was on the floor, I couldn’t see what was happening to my students. We were not a charismatic group. We were open to the Holy Spirit, but we didn’t have experiences like speaking in tongues or being “slain in the Spirit.” As he called the youth to the altar, leaders were told to come forward as well. I expected the usual—kids crying, kneeling, praying. But when I reached the front, I froze. My students were hyperventilating. Some looked terrified. Others were on the edge of panic. This was not normal.

Then the speaker began “ministering.” He laid hands on kids, blew on them, shouted “MORE!” as they fell over. Some screamed. Some shook violently. The atmosphere turned dark—deeply wrong—and I hated every second of it. But I had lost control. Four leaders for thirty scattered students. I didn’t know where half of them were. I just prayed for God to have mercy.

After what felt like an hour, the lights suddenly snapped on. The music stopped. The speaker was gone. Later I learned the camp director had shut it down because he realized it was out of control. God bless him.

Then came the aftermath.

One leader took the kids who were okay to the dining hall to debrief. My wife and I handled the rest. I found one girl curled on the floor in the fetal position, saying she felt like she was burning inside. I picked her up—bad knee and all—and carried her to a row of chairs. Another student twisted her neck and insisted she had demons. I told her firmly she didn’t, that none of this was from God, and we stayed with her until she calmed down. Two students were completely unresponsive—eyes open, but unmoving and silent. Hours later, one finally spoke, saying he felt crushed under the guilt of his sin, convinced he was damned with no hope of forgiveness. The other never spoke to me again and never returned to youth group. Several students never came back at all.

I want to show you a video that illustrates the burning feeling the girl was talking about. This video shows a similar situation happening at Bethel Church, where a man literally begins rolling on the floor screaming while the leader calls "Fire!" down upon his head. You might think this is unbelieveable, but this burning feeling must be real as I saw it in the one girl in my youth group.

When we joined the others in the dining hall, I didn’t sugarcoat anything. I told them plainly that what they had witnessed was wrong and not Christian. This sparked immediate controversy. Another youth leader confronted me, insisting the night had been “powerful,” asking, “Don’t you believe in the power of God?” I nearly punched him. Parents began calling, demanding answers. I was up until 3 a.m. comforting students, calming parents, and dealing with my own shock. The worship band was so furious they packed up their gear and left in the middle of the snowstorm. The speaker disappeared and was never heard from again.

The next morning, a volunteer led simple worship and shared a straightforward gospel message. Many were in tears, including me. It felt like a small light had returned to the world.

We met with my pastor to tell him everything that had happened. It was disappointing. Although he agreed that the incident was horrible, there was no real follow‑up, no clear condemnation, no sense of pastoral responsibility. I spoke with several others—the camp director, people from my church—and the response was the same. No blame. No accountability. Just the idea that nobody could have foreseen what happened. And yet I kept wishing others saw it the way I did: as something unquestionably wrong.

Instead, I heard phrases like “eat the meat, leave the bones,” meaning take whatever good you can from a message and discard the bad. But I couldn’t understand why we were allowing someone to preach a message with “badness” in it at all. Another analogy came to mind: if someone poisoned your milkshake, would you tell people to drink the shake and just leave the poison? Of course not. In that case, the evil is inseparable. And I found myself wondering which analogy better described what had actually happened.

Someone else told me, “Well, we’re a Holy Spirit movement, and you just have to be open to whatever the Holy Spirit is going to do.” This idea of living spiritually with no guardrails, no authority, no discernment—just openness to anything—I was repulsed by it. In fact, I started putting the pieces together and realizing that this was the inevitable result of people chasing an “authentic” spiritual life without any real tools or methods for finding it. This is how you get Soul Care. This is how charismatic influence infects a church. This is how you end up with Bethel music shaping your worship.

The disillusionment I felt in the weeks after that horrible event cannot be overstated. And that’s not even counting the parents’ meeting I had to lead afterward, trying once again to calm everyone down and make sense of something that should never have happened in the first place.

I also started to feel a bit sorry for myself. I kept thinking, I’m an unpaid volunteer—why am I the one dealing with all of this? I’ll admit I felt a little victimized, and maybe that wasn’t entirely fair. But I didn’t have much time to dwell on it, because we were heading into the biggest event these youth group kids would experience during their time with me: LIFE 2025, the national C&MA youth conference in Indianapolis.

The preparation nearly broke me. I handled registration, medical and insurance forms, payments, booking a rental vehicle, reserving hotel rooms, securing conference tickets, arranging my own PTO from work, finding childcare for my little kids, assembling an emergency trauma kit, and countless other logistical tasks. By the time the day arrived, I had almost nothing left in the tank. Still, we piled into a 15‑passenger van and drove eight hours from central Pennsylvania to Indianapolis for a week at the conference.

But by then, I was already disillusioned—by the endless rededication cycles, by worship music, by preaching, by dim lights and fog machines, by rock‑concert aesthetics, by corporate church culture, and by evangelicalism as a whole. I led almost passively when it came to the spiritual side of the event, though I remained fully engaged in caring for the kids and keeping everyone safe. I knew my ties to the C&MA were fraying, and I knew I was about to witness the denomination’s “best of the best”—the president, the top speakers, the most celebrated leaders.

And I was so disappointed by what I saw. Every talk, every seminar, every worship song revolved around identity in Christ, about how we are who God says we are, about how God loves us. But it was all vague and generic—positive, encouraging, “Jesusy” language with no substance. No talk of repentance. No talk of the cross. No call to die to ourselves and live to Him. Just self‑help dressed up in Christian vocabulary.

Worse still, the spiritual buffet continued. Seminars on obtaining the Holy Spirit’s power. Sessions where leaders laid hands on kids to impart healing abilities. And then, the worst part: the re‑opening of trauma from the retreat earlier that year. When one leader mentioned the Holy Spirit and “power,” one of my students actually jumped out of her seat and clung to me, crying, terrified that the whole thing was about to turn into another disaster like the one we had already endured. I refused to subject my kids to that again, and we left several sessions early.

It was clear: the charismatic infection had metastasized throughout the entire denomination.

By the end of LIFE, I knew my home was no longer in the C&MA. My own church wasn’t as extreme—our pastor was more grounded—but he wouldn’t be there forever. And I had serious questions to face. What legacy was I handing to my kids? They wouldn’t be under my roof much longer. What kind of faith was I giving them? One driven by emotional cycles, by attempts to gain power from the Holy Spirit, by cultural trends, by Bethel‑influenced worship and theology?

The only problem was that if the C&MA was no longer home… I had no idea where I was supposed to go next.

As I entered my fourth and final year as a youth leader, I was already looking for an off‑ramp from the ministry. I felt confident the other leaders could carry on without me, and I sensed that my own outlook and personality no longer fit the direction the youth group was heading. The group had gotten younger, and I’ve never connected as naturally with middle‑school students. After everything I had experienced the previous year, I was simply ready to be done. Internally, I had already decided this would be my last year.

I didn’t tell anyone yet, mostly because I was worried about disappointing the kids I had ministered to for years. We had gone to the altar together, shared struggles, worshiped, and had those deep campfire conversations. I was afraid my leaving would hurt or confuse them. Looking back, maybe there was a bit of pride mixed in—thinking I was too important to step away. But the truth is that I was out of energy, and a new activity pushed me past my limit, leaving me feeling like I just didn’t care anymore.

We got an intern—God bless him—who had a strong heart for outreach and preaching the gospel to the kids in his school. He introduced an event called the Fifth Quarter: an open‑gym hangout after home football games where kids could stay until midnight. We regularly had over seventy students show up, and it was borderline out of control. I was filling Gatorade coolers, working the popcorn machine, cleaning messes, guarding hallways, trying to prevent damage, and more. It was absolutely exhausting, and there were nights I wanted to cry when it was finally over. But the exhaustion wasn’t only physical. I no longer shared the evangelical worldview that shaped the ministry, and continuing to participate so deeply in something I no longer believed in was soul‑crushing. Still, I loved the kids, and I was genuinely committed to finishing out the year with them.

The final breaking point came when one of the youth group students invited me to her baptism. I was confused, because I hadn’t heard anything from the pastor about an upcoming service. When I started asking questions, I learned that she didn’t want to be baptized in the church at all. Instead, she had asked a fellow youth group student to baptize her, and he had agreed. The date was set for the very next day. I was shocked. I immediately thought this was a terrible idea.

I called the young man to confirm what I had heard, and he told me it was all true. I urged him to call the pastor, and he told me he already had. The pastor had reacted the same way I did, telling him not to go through with it. But the young man had decided that, according to his personal interpretation of the Bible, “pastor” was just a man‑made word, and any believer could baptize anyone else. In his mind, that settled it.

I knew what the future could hold. This girl might look back someday and realize she had made a mistake—one she couldn’t undo. She had no preparation, no guidance, and the whole thing was a spontaneous emotional decision. I’m not saying baptisms can never be spontaneous, but everything about this felt wrong. And I had learned to trust that instinct, because I had seen the damage that spiritual chaos could cause in the lives of teenagers.

I pleaded with him not to do it. He didn’t listen. The next day, the baptism happened. Almost no one showed up. It was underwhelming. I wasn’t there, and neither was the pastor. And in that moment, I remember deciding with absolute clarity: I am finished with youth leading. Period. I didn’t care what happened next or even if they begged me to stay. I was done for good.

In fact, I wasn’t just done with youth leading. I was done with spiritual chaos. I was done with “formation” that had no foundation in truth. I was done with the rededication cycle and the spiritual buffet. I was done being an evangelical. I began preparing myself and my family for a transition into a new faith—one I had been quietly researching, studying, and practicing at home for two years: Eastern Orthodox Christianity.
