title: Coming Home
permalink: /coming-home/

In November 2025, God answered my prayer. While we were in Pittsburgh for the weekend, my wife made the suggestion I had been hoping for: “Hey, why don’t we visit an Orthodox church while we’re here?” Of course I said yes. I was excited but also nervous, unsure whether my first Divine Liturgy would live up to the expectations I had built over the previous two years. I had tied my heart to a faith I had never actually experienced. Would it all be a disappointment? And if so, what would that mean? Where else could I go? I knew something would change forever after this visit.

We arrived at Saint Alexander Nevsky Cathedral on November 9, 2025. As soon as we stepped inside, the smell of incense hit me. Icons were everywhere. I heard the reader chanting the hours. The atmosphere was reverent and quiet. I can’t fully explain it, but I immediately knew I was home—before the liturgy even began. After the service, the priest’s wife invited us downstairs for coffee hour, and we accepted. Father John sat with us, answered our questions, and offered encouragement for our journey.

It wasn’t until we were back in the van heading home that I finally asked my wife what she thought. She choked up as she tried to answer: “It was so beautiful. It felt like these people actually believe Christ is in their midst. I was reminded of the Christians and martyrs while watching them worship and take communion.” My children also had mostly positive reactions, especially my oldest son. After the trauma of the retreat from hell, he may have been even more eager than I was for a sober faith and to worship God in spirit and truth.

I told my family to keep the trip a secret. My instincts warned me that if anyone at our current church learned we were exploring Orthodoxy, it would change our situation—especially since I was serving as a youth leader. I wasn’t teaching anything contrary to the C&MA statement of faith, but still, my instincts told me to keep this quiet, and so I did.

I searched my local area for Orthodox churches and found one about thirty minutes from our home. I reached out to someone I knew in the parish to ask whether it would be okay to visit, and he assured me we would be welcome. So I took my youngest daughter to Great Vespers on a Saturday night in December. It happened to be the feast of the Entrance of the Theotokos into the Temple, which felt fitting for my own first entrance into what I hoped would become my new spiritual home. The service was beautiful, and it was a joy to see that the faith and practice of this small parish in the middle of nowhere matched what I had experienced at the large cathedral in the city. This was what I longed for: consistency, truth, continuity, fear of God, and true worship—and here it was in rural Pennsylvania.

Several parishioners stopped me that evening and engaged me in a long conversation. I was encouraged to learn that many of them were converts, and that I was not alone in my journey out of evangelicalism. The priest himself was a convert, just like the one I had met in Pittsburgh, which I hadn’t expected. He told me that my family and I were welcome anytime, and that we were already considered part of the parish even after just one visit. He and I began meeting for lunch and getting to know each other. I told him about my commitment as a youth leader, and he encouraged me to see those commitments through, to be patient, and to let God handle everything. His advice helped cool down my sense of urgency.

Because we had obligations on Sunday mornings, we began attending Vespers on Saturday nights. We were able to attend Divine Liturgy on Christmas morning, since my own church didn’t hold services on Christmas Day unless it fell on a Sunday. Maria and I both cried during the Christmas Liturgy because of the joy present in the worship. One unexpected blessing was reconnecting with my old high school band teacher. My wife and I had always loved him, but we hadn’t seen him in years—and now here we were, reunited in the context of worship. It was very special.

Eventually it became difficult to keep all of this a secret, and I realized it wasn’t fair or right to continue doing so. I knew I had one foot in two different camps, so to speak, and I needed to choose one. I began speaking with my family about converting to the Orthodox Church. My wife and oldest son were completely on board. My two youngest children were not yet at an age where the decision would be theirs, but they enjoyed the new church and the people, and they loved the new ways we were beginning to worship God at home—especially using icons in prayer and praying together with set prayers. They naturally seemed to love the Theotokos. My priest explained that kids “get it.” They don’t need to be taught nearly as much as adult converts need to unlearn so many things. In other words, we adults should try to be like them. And he was right.

My oldest daughter struggled more. In terms of faith and practice she was on board with the Orthodox Church, but the thought of being separated from her friends was enough to threaten to undo everything. I deeply hoped we would all convert as a family, but I had to admit she was old enough to make this decision for herself. She wasn’t opposed to the faith—only to losing her friends. So we worked out a plan that, with our priest’s blessing, she would continue attending the summer camp she grew up in and would still go to family camp as well. This was simply to reassure her that we weren’t trying to break relationships and that we understood how she felt. She agreed, and the decision was made. We were going to enroll as catechumens.

But I had to speak with my pastor first. I was still committed to finishing out the year as a youth leader, and I told my priest that we wanted to delay entering the catechumenate until the school year was over. He agreed, but he also said, “I think you will be here sooner than you expect. They will probably ask you to be done immediately.” I told him no—I didn’t think that would happen. I felt he didn’t understand how strong our relationships were and how connected we were to the youth group. But it turns out I was naive, and probably had a higher view of my own importance than I should have had.

I met with my pastor for lunch and told him our plans: that after the end of this year’s youth group, my entire family and I would become Orthodox Christians. He said he knew something was going on with me but was waiting for me to come out and say it. The Orthodox part was probably a big surprise. I tried to explain some of my reasons, but as I said earlier in this document, how do you reduce a lifetime journey and years of searching into a one‑hour conversation? It’s truly impossible. In the end, I think the conversation fell flat, but to his credit my pastor told me he was trusting the Holy Spirit to guide my family, and that we would always be welcome back at our old church. I felt pretty upbeat after the meeting and thought things were going well.

But later that week we were called in to meet with the elders, and that was the moment we had to face some pain. Looking back, I don’t blame anyone for this; in fact, I’m thankful for what happened because it allowed us to go where we were going a little more quickly. But we were informed, in no uncertain terms, that if we were truly Orthodox in our hearts, we would no longer be allowed to serve in the youth group, on the worship team, in the nursery—basically at all. Though the evangelical church doesn’t have the word for this, I now understand what happened: we were being excommunicated. Or perhaps it’s better to say we excommunicated ourselves by the decision we had made. But I can’t deny that it hurt.

I wept for days in my prayer corner after this happened. I wept harder than I have over anything, except perhaps the death of my grandmother. I wept until I felt my eyes were going to burst. But I also felt the presence of God—a subtle yet undeniable presence in my heart. I felt the compassion of the Most Holy Theotokos, my first real experience of the Mother of God in my life. I can’t explain these feelings. There is a mystery in all of this. All I can say is that I knew it was real. It felt like mourning a death. But I came out the other side ready to embrace the light and life of the apostolic faith, to worship God in spirit and truth.

We met with our priest, and I had another weeping session as he comforted me. He said we could become catechumens. Our time as catechumens would be relatively short, but he said we had already made an offering to God through everything that had happened. We were to experience the fullness of Great Lent and Holy Week, and then, for the fifty days after Pascha, learn through the catechetical lectures of Saint Cyril of Jerusalem. We did all of these things.

It would take too much time to describe our experience of Lent and Holy Week, and this document is already long. Suffice it to say that I felt truly present through all the key moments—Palm Sunday, the Last Supper, the crucifixion and burial of Christ, singing “Let God Arise” on Holy Saturday. It felt like being present in those events in real time. The fasting was difficult; I learned a lot about myself and how irritable I can be when I don’t get my worldly desires met whenever I want. But it was done in community, and seeing the beauties of the Canon of Saint Andrew, the Presanctified Liturgies, the late‑night coffee hours, the grueling three‑hour services—sometimes twice a day—being done all together, I was immersing myself in the Orthodox mindset and giving God all the glory the entire time.

Pascha was such a joy—shouting the resurrection of Christ at two in the morning, then feasting together afterward, eating all the delicious foods we had refrained from for a month and a half. Singing “The Angel Cried”—I swear all the icons in the room were smiling. I have come to see little miracles happen often during the Divine Liturgy, and they no longer surprise me, but the first time it happened while singing that hymn, I was amazed, like a child. So much beauty, truth, and goodness. Why was I found worthy to discover this faith?

We finished our last few weeks of catechumenate and were received into the One, Holy, Catholic, and Apostolic Church through baptism and chrismation on Pentecost—the day we celebrate the coming of the Holy Spirit and the birth of the Church. It was an appropriate day for us to be sealed with the Spirit. The liturgy was long, almost three hours, but it passed in a blur. To come into the Church together, as a family, was my greatest joy.

Taking my first communion, it literally felt like a bomb went off in my mouth as I partook of the Body and Blood of Christ for the first time. I stumbled from the chalice over to the side table to receive the blessed bread and wine; I didn’t even know where I was. So many other things happened on that great day of celebration, including a graduation party at my priest’s house that night. It was a full day of joy. It was the best day of my life.

Since then, I have continued trying to adopt the mind of Christ as my emotions have cooled and I begin to participate in the steady rhythms of fasting, confession, communion, and worship. I praise God for everything He has done in my life.

I will conclude this document by declaring the thanksgiving we sing after every Divine Liturgy:

We have seen the True Light! We have received the Heavenly Spirit! We have found the True Faith! Worshiping the Undivided Trinity, Who has saved us.

Let our mouths be filled with Thy praise, O Lord, that we may sing of Thy glory; for Thou hast made us worthy to partake of Thy Holy, Divine, Immortal, and Life‑creating Mysteries. Keep us in Thy holiness, that all the day we may meditate upon Thy righteousness.

Alleluia! Alleluia! Alleluia!
