Getting back to my story, it was really in youth group where I was first exposed to revivalism as a method of spiritual formation. It started with that first retreat I already mentioned, with my first time going to the altar and my first time making a decision for Christ. It became an integral part of who I was as a Christian. In fact, I almost couldn’t behave in a Christian manner outside of the revivalist system. My normal Christian life became a life punctuated by dramatic moments, altar calls, emotional decisions for Christ, and powerful encounters with what I thought was the Holy Spirit. Perhaps it was; I don’t want to judge those experiences. All I can say is that it was something I had to constantly renew, reaffirm, or recommit to.

Often at these altar calls, the speaker would make two invitations: one for those who wished to commit their life to Christ for the first time, which usually brought a few people down to the altar, and then a second invitation for those of us who were already saved, giving us an opportunity to rededicate ourselves to Christ. We all knew what happened: we went to these retreats, worshiped intensely, experienced powerful emotions and bodily sensations we had never felt outside of that atmosphere, attributed it to the Holy Spirit, got a spiritual high, and felt like we were on fire for God. Some of these retreats were even called Acquire the Fire. Then they sent us home, and we returned to real life with the same temptations, the same internal passions, the same struggles, the same friend groups, the same home problems, and the same cravings. It didn’t take long before we encountered what I call the spiritual slump—tumbling down off the mountaintop of spiritual high into the valley of sin.

Now I wasn’t just a sinner; I was in despair because I had grasped a brief glimpse of God, let it slip through my fingers, and returned to the same person I always was, or even worse than before. I remember going to these events, getting the spiritual high, and then coming back and hanging out with youth group kids, cruising around town and smoking cigarettes. I personally only smoked one, thought it was disgusting, and gave up on it. But the point was that Christ was not the center of my life. He was a “Sunday” category. He was not all‑encompassing. He was not all in all. He was not who he was supposed to be for me.

Part of the problem with this revivalist system is that when kids get home, there is usually no follow‑up unless they show up on Sunday mornings for Sunday school. There is no discipleship, no relationship with a spiritual father or mother to be accountable to, except for maybe a few lucky ones. Most were on their own, and their only connection to any sort of discipleship or accountability was a few hours on Sunday morning or Sunday evening. We had youth group, but that was it.

Sometimes at these retreats I would feel the desire to go up to the altar but would be too embarrassed to be seen, and I wouldn’t go. These were the worst, because when I got home I felt not only despair but also fear of damnation, believing I had let my fear of people keep me from going up to do business with God. I would see people raise their hands in worship and want to do the same, but I also felt somewhat repulsed by it and didn’t want to put my hands up. I feared that I was being ashamed of God and that He would judge me for not raising my hands. There were also times I didn’t feel anything at all, and I wondered if I was so far gone that God didn’t want to meet with me anymore.

The only practical guidance I ever received was to read my Bible and pray. While reading the Bible and praying is good, it is only a small portion of the tools that could have helped us live a consistent Christian life. Most of the tools we were given were intellectual—Bible studies, memorizing verses, discussing Scripture, small groups, reading books together. This led to another phenomenon in my life: because I was somewhat intellectual and good at reading and understanding arguments, people started to think I was very spiritual. I could talk well about Scripture or about the book we were reading, and somehow intellectual ability became equated with spiritual maturity. But the truth was that in the background of my life, I had no Christian life. I had no daily prayer. I had no connection with God whatsoever, other than strong feelings as I continued to chase revivals and retreats, hoping to capture a feeling that would last and not slip through my fingers. I remained on the rededication cycle for many years, even up until the year before I converted to Orthodoxy.

These retreats were intense. The lights were dim, the music was loud and often somewhat sensual. People cried at the altar. Boys and girls hugged each other, and I often felt uncomfortable realizing that a girl was touching me while I was trying to pour out my soul to God, distracted by the physical elements of it. I brushed up against Pentecostal and charismatic expressions like speaking in tongues and falling on the floor, and I was completely repulsed by it. It was one of the most horrible feelings I ever had in my heart. I was angry and never wanted to experience it again. Thankfully, in my teen years the Pentecostal/charismatic element was not very strong in the C&MA, although that has changed now.

I remember at one Christian music festival spontaneously getting baptized again, crying my heart out and hoping that this time the baptism would “stick,” because I felt so defiled and dirty. I just wanted to be clean and pure again and pleasing in the sight of God. No matter how hard I tried to grab hold of Him at these retreats, it slipped through my fingers every time. So I continued to chase these spiritual highs. They came, they faded, and I stayed the same. Despite all the altar calls, commitments, rededications, retreats, and emotional experiences, I never became the kind of person who could consistently live as a Christian on an ordinary Tuesday afternoon.
