Getting back to my story, it was really in youth group where I was first exposed to revivalism as a method of spiritual formation. It started with that first retreat I already mentioned, with my first time going to the altar and my first time making a decision for Christ. It became an integral part of who I was as a Christian. In fact, I almost couldn’t behave in a Christian manner outside of the revivalist system. My normal Christian life became a life punctuated by dramatic moments, altar calls, emotional decisions for Christ, and powerful encounters with what I thought was the Holy Spirit. Perhaps it was; I don’t want to judge those experiences. All I can say is that it was something I had to constantly renew, reaffirm, or recommit to.

These retreats were intense. The lights were dim, the music was loud, and emotions ran high. People cried at the altar. Boys and girls hugged one another while praying, and I often found myself distracted by the physical and emotional atmosphere around me when I was trying to pour out my soul to God. Instead of helping me focus on God, it sometimes pulled my attention toward everything else that was happening around me. The video below shows an example of an altar call at a youth event. This one looks like it is happening in a safe atmosphere which is good. Without the right leadership, though, these things can begin to get out of control, as you will hear about later on in this memoir.

Often at these altar calls, the speaker would make two invitations: one for those who wished to commit their life to Christ for the first time, and another for those of us who were already Christians and wanted to rededicate our lives to Him. Most of us responded to the second call. Over time, the cycle became predictable. We would go to a retreat, worship intensely, hear powerful preaching, pray with tears, and leave believing our lives had been changed. For a few days or weeks we felt spiritually alive. Then real life returned. The same temptations were there. The same sins were there. The same weaknesses were there. The emotions faded, the enthusiasm cooled, and we found ourselves needing another retreat, another altar call, and another rededication. I came to know this pattern well. It was a continual cycle of spiritual highs followed by spiritual slumps.

Now I wasn't just a sinner; I was in despair because I had grasped a brief glimpse of God, let it slip through my fingers, and returned to the same person I had always been, or even worse than before. I remember going to these events, experiencing the spiritual high, and then coming home and hanging out with youth group friends, cruising around town, swearing, listening to filthy music, and falling back into the same patterns as before. It felt as though nothing had really changed. Christ was not the center of my life. He belonged in a "Sunday" category. He was not all-encompassing. He was not all in all. He was not who He was supposed to be for me.

Sometimes at these retreats I would feel the desire to go up to the altar, but I would be too embarrassed to be seen, so I stayed in my seat. Those were the worst ones. When I got home, I felt not only despair but also fear, believing I had allowed my fear of people to keep me from responding to God. I thought I had missed my opportunity to do business with Him.

I would see people raising their hands during worship and feel torn. Part of me wanted to join them, but another part of me felt uncomfortable and even somewhat repulsed by it. Because I wouldn't raise my hands, I worried that I was ashamed of God and that He would judge me for it. There were also times when everyone around me seemed deeply moved while I felt nothing at all. On those occasions I wondered whether I had drifted so far from God that He no longer wanted to meet with me. If I felt the right things, I believed God was near. If I didn't, I wondered whether He had abandoned me.

Part of the problem with this revivalist system was that when kids returned home, there was usually little follow-up beyond Sunday school or youth group. There was no real discipleship, no spiritual father or mother to guide us and hold us accountable, except perhaps for a fortunate few. Occasionally some of us would decide to form "accountability groups" where we could confess our sins to one another and pray together, but the enthusiasm rarely lasted long, and most of those efforts fizzled out. For the most part, we were on our own. Our only connection to discipleship and accountability consisted of a few hours on Sunday morning and a few more hours at youth group during the week. Beyond that, we were largely left to navigate the Christian life by ourselves.

I remember at one Christian music festival spontaneously getting baptized again, crying my heart out and hoping that this time the baptism would “stick,” because I felt so defiled and dirty. I just wanted to be clean and pure again and pleasing in the sight of God. No matter how hard I tried to grab hold of Him at these retreats, it slipped through my fingers every time. So I continued to chase these spiritual highs. They came, they faded, and I stayed the same. Despite all the altar calls, commitments, rededications, retreats, and emotional experiences, I never became the kind of person who could consistently live as a Christian on an ordinary Tuesday afternoon.

The only practical guidance I ever received was to read my Bible and pray. Reading the Bible and praying are good things, but they were presented to us as almost the entire Christian life. Most of the formation we received was intellectual: Bible studies, Scripture memory, small groups, book discussions, and learning how to think about the faith. These things had value, but they left a significant gap.

This created another problem in my life. Because I was naturally intellectual and good at reading, understanding arguments, and discussing ideas, people began to think I was spiritually mature. I knew the right answers. I could talk about Scripture, contribute to Bible studies, and discuss whatever Christian book we were reading. Somehow intellectual ability became confused with spiritual growth.

But the reality was very different. Behind the scenes, I had little real Christian life. I had no daily prayer rule, no consistent discipline of repentance, and no regular practice of seeking God. My spiritual life consisted largely of chasing powerful experiences at retreats and revivals, hoping to capture a feeling that would finally last. The feelings would come and go, but I remained largely unchanged. So I stayed on the cycle of spiritual highs, spiritual slumps, and repeated rededications for many years, right up until the year before I converted to Orthodoxy.
