Dispensationalism is an all‑encompassing way of viewing history, where the entire timeline is already mapped out and you’re simply living at a point on that chart—always on the verge of prophecy being fulfilled. Current events are interpreted as signs pointing toward the end times. This framework includes the idea of *The Rapture*, a secret return of Christ in which he whisks away the Christians before the end time tribulation begins. It teaches that Israel plays a critical role in the fulfillment of prophecy and that Israel and the Church are distinct entities in God’s plan. It also comes with a whole vocabulary of end‑times concepts—millennium, tribulation, antichrist, mark of the beast—familiar to anyone who grew up with books like Left Behind, The Late Great Planet Earth, or even the Scofield Reference Bible.

I cannot overstate this fact: dispensationalism was the theological air I breathed growing up, even though I didn’t know that word at the time. One side of my family was especially focused on the end times. My grandfather had an entire bookshelf full of prophecy books. We were warned that the rapture could happen at any moment and that we had to be ready for it; otherwise, we could be left behind. This was confusing, because I had invited Jesus into my heart many times and was supposedly safe, yet I lived with a constant fear that I might still be doomed. I was never fully assured that I was sincere enough in my belief. If Jesus was in my heart, why did I have to be ready for the rapture? Wasn’t I already ready? As you will see throughout this document, tensions, contradictions, and crises like these were always present in my faith life.

I remember one afternoon coming home from school to an empty house. My parents had gone shopping and hadn’t returned before the bus dropped me off, and when I realized I was alone, I went into a full panic. I was convinced the rapture had happened and I had been left behind. I went to their rooms to see if there were clothes on the floor where they might have been standing—one of the supposed signs that the rapture had occurred—and turned on the TV to look for reports of car crashes everywhere, planes falling from the sky, and other disasters that would follow millions of Christians suddenly disappearing. I felt sick to my stomach as I imagined the seven years of tribulation I was about to endure. How would I survive? What about the Mark of the Beast? My mind went wild. When my parents finally walked through the door, I burst into tears, completely overwhelmed. They laughed, probably thinking it looked cute from their perspective, and maybe because they had felt the same fear themselves when they were young. But I remember it not being funny at all. It had never even occurred to me to check whether their car was in the driveway or if they had simply gone out.

We were warned constantly about the Mark of the Beast. We understood the Antichrist to be a charismatic figure—probably a politician—who would establish a new world order and a new economy in which people would need to receive some kind of barcode-like mark on their forehead or right hand in order to buy or sell anything. We were convinced the Antichrist was already alive and on the scene, simply waiting to reveal himself, and we believed the technology was already in place to implement the Mark of the Beast globally. It could be hidden in barcodes, credit cards, or some other emerging technology. It was terrifying, because receiving the Mark meant eternal damnation with no possibility of forgiveness. Although the Bible says the only unforgivable sin is blasphemy against the Holy Spirit, we were taught that after the rapture God had essentially abandoned the world, leaving no chance for repentance. In that context, the Mark of the Beast became an unforgivable sin that would send you straight to hell, and there was nothing you could do to remove it. We watched movies like A Thief in the Night that scared the living daylights out of me, showing people who had received the Mark realizing what they had done and being filled with terror at the doom awaiting them at the end of the tribulation. Even the number 666 itself was treated as evil, something you shouldn’t say, and encountering it in daily life was considered an ill omen.

We watched TV shows about something called the Bible Code: the idea that God had hidden secret messages in Scripture. Supposedly, these messages could be uncovered by following certain patterns, such as skipping every few letters or tracing crossword-like and acrostic sequences through the text.

What always bothered me was that the rules seemed to change. Sometimes the codes were said to be hidden in the original Greek manuscripts. Other times they appeared in the English Bible. The methods for finding them were never clear.

According to the Bible Code movement, names such as Winston Churchill, Napoleon, John F. Kennedy, and even events like September 11, 2001, or the end of World War II could be found hidden within Scripture. Phrases like “behold the blood of Jesus” supposedly appeared as well.

Secret numbers were everywhere. Seven, twelve, forty, 144,000, and 666 were all believed to carry deeper mystical meanings that we were meant to decipher. It always felt as though there was another layer of hidden knowledge lurking behind the plain reading of the Bible.

Even as a child, something about that troubled me. If these hidden messages were so important, why did I have to buy someone's book or video series to learn about them? That drove me crazy. It felt as though we needed this knowledge, yet access to it depended on whether we could afford it.

We watched television prophets like Jack Van Impe, who explained the end times in great detail and offered books and video series to help us prepare. Growing up, I was only allowed to stay up late on Saturday nights if I watched Jack Van Impe Presents with my parents. So there I would be, watching Jack Van Impe connect current events and newspaper headlines to the Book of Revelation, convinced that every new development in the world was a sign that the end was near. He always had a big smile on his face, as if to say, “Yes, it’s almost here. Yes, Lord, come quickly,” and his wife Rexella would sit beside him, encouraging it all. The strange thing was that so much of his joy and excitement came from terrible situations in the world—war, famine, terrorist attacks in Israel or elsewhere in the Middle East, Russia being aggressive. These were all things that were bad for the world, yet he looked on them with glee because he believed they were ushering in the rapture. I was completely engrossed in this. I was excited, and I was all in for everything he preached. Again, I wished we had the money to buy the video series and books, but we didn’t, and that made me feel like we were missing out on the “extra good” prophecy. And yet, whenever I wondered when the actual rapture was going to occur, it always seemed imminent—and it never happened.

Let me give you a strange example of the absurdity of dispensationalist theology. As a child, I was told that I probably would not have to worry about dying because Christ would return first. The reasoning was based on a popular interpretation of Matthew 24:34. Dispensationalists often taught that the "generation" that would not pass away before Christ's return was the generation alive when the modern state of Israel was founded in 1948. Since a generation was commonly defined as only 50 to 60 years, many expected the rapture to occur sometime around the turn of the millennium. Born in 1986, I was told I was one of the "lucky" ones. The expectation was that I would likely be raptured before reaching adulthood, never having to face death at all. As a result, I genuinely believed, at least on some level, that I was exempt from mortality. It sounds bizarre now, but that belief made coming to terms with my own mortality later in life much more difficult than it should have been.

Dispensationalism shaped our politics very strongly. Our support for Israel was absolute and unquestioning because we believed modern events were directly tied to biblical prophecy and that God had made special promises to the modern state of Israel. When we heard about Russia or Iran, we immediately thought of Gog and Magog from Ezekiel. As a result, we reflexively supported politicians based on two issues: support for Israel and abortion. Those are legitimate issues to consider, but we were willing to overlook major economic decisions—like factories being sent overseas—that harmed the country, as long as the politician aligned with our hot‑button concerns.

My parents sent significant amounts of money to Messianic Jewish ministries and to groups claiming to be preparing to rebuild the Third Temple in Jerusalem. This was during a time when we often didn’t have reliable transportation or enough money for shoes. I don’t blame them—they were sincere. They believed they were participating in God’s end‑time plan, and at the time, I thought so too. But it shows how deeply theology can shape everyday life. These ideas weren’t just theoretical; they had practical consequences.

Even as a child, this theology didn’t make sense to me. When I sat down and read the Bible alone, I would look at the important verses—like the one in Matthew that says a generation would not pass away, or the verses in Thessalonians about being caught up in the air to meet the Lord, and many others like them. What bothered me immensely was that when you rip these verses out of the Bible in an isolated, de‑contextualized fashion, you can sort of glue them together and build a theory like dispensationalism. But when you simply sit down and read the books as books—reading Matthew from beginning to end, or reading the whole letter to the Thessalonians—without pulling verses out of context and instead letting them speak within their actual setting, dispensationalism becomes much harder to make sense of.

These verses were interpreted for me through the guidance and authority of people I trusted: Jack Van Impe, other television prophets, my parents, preachers in the Christian and Missionary Alliance, and other denominations. I didn’t know this as a kid, but you are always reading Scripture through a lens of interpretation. You cannot sit down and “just read the Bible” without a lens, because you already have a worldview, a way of understanding things, and that will always be in play. Sola scriptura—the idea that all we need to do is read the Bible and it will speak for itself—is unfortunately not true. So the real question becomes: who is your authority? What is your interpretive framework? Whom are you going to submit to when trying to understand the Bible?

I remember sitting in study hall in high school with a Bible by myself, deciding that I was going to figure out the “correct” Christianity. I quickly realized this would be absolutely impossible and came to the disheartening conclusion that maybe there was no true Christianity at all, and that we were all just trying to figure it out because God had decided not to tell us. Now, as an Eastern Orthodox Christian, I have chosen to submit my rational mind—at least in terms of interpreting Scripture—to the Church Fathers and the faith of the apostles that has been handed down. Of course, I understood none of this back then. All I knew was that there was a tension in my heart because the rapture simply didn’t make sense when I read the Bible.
